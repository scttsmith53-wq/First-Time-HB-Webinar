What Colorado buyers should include in a homebuying budget

By Scott Smith, Colorado mortgage loan originator and real estate broker

Before you start comparing homes in Denver or northern Colorado, write down what you want homeownership to cost each month, how much cash you can put into the purchase, and what you want to keep in savings. Those numbers give you a useful starting point for conversations with your lender and real estate agent.

A down-payment percentage or an online mortgage estimate can help you begin. To build a budget you can use, you also need the costs tied to the property, the loan, and the move. Here is how to put them together.

Start with the full monthly cost

Your principal and interest payment covers repayment of the loan and the cost of borrowing. Your monthly mortgage bill may also include mortgage insurance and an escrow payment for property taxes and homeowners insurance. Check the written estimate to see what is included. [1]

If taxes or insurance are not escrowed, you still need to budget for them. HOA dues are usually paid separately. Even with a fixed mortgage interest rate, taxes and insurance can change, so the total payment can change too. [1]

Add utilities and a realistic allowance for maintenance and repairs. Then look at what remains for groceries, transportation, debt payments, childcare, savings, and the other things your household pays for. The goal is a monthly amount you are comfortable living with. [5]

Ask your lender: “Which costs are included in this payment, and which would I pay separately?” Write the answer next to the estimate so you do not leave out a bill or count it twice.

Separate your down payment from cash to close

The down payment is one part of the money needed to buy. Closing costs may include lender charges, appraisal and title-related fees, government charges, prepaid expenses, and initial escrow funding. The mix depends on the loan and transaction. [2, 6]

On the Loan Estimate, review the Estimated Cash to Close calculation. It accounts for the down payment and closing costs, then adjusts for items such as deposits already paid, seller credits, and other applicable adjustments. Ask your lender to explain each line rather than adding the down payment to a rough closing-cost estimate yourself. [2]

Also ask when money is due. Keep a separate list of deposits and any inspection, appraisal, or other expenses you will pay before closing. Confirm how payments already made are reflected in the closing figures so you do not budget for the same expense twice.

If credits or assistance are part of the plan, ask what is confirmed, what is still conditional, and how the loan or program terms affect your costs. Keep unconfirmed amounts separate from funds you know are available.

Check the actual Colorado property

Replace general estimates with information for the home you are considering. Ask for an insurance quote for that address, review the property tax information, and confirm any HOA dues. A lower tax or insurance estimate on one loan offer does not necessarily make that loan less expensive. Those costs are not set by the lender. [2, 4]

Ask whether the property is in a metropolitan district. These districts can add property taxes, and a neighborhood can have both an HOA and a metro district. The Town of Erie advises buyers to include the district tax in their budget and warns that a new home's early tax bill may not yet reflect it. Confirm what the property's estimate includes rather than assuming the current bill represents future costs. [7]

Keep the district tax within your property-tax calculation when it is already included there. List separate HOA dues and other charges separately.

Keep money for the move and the months after it

Before deciding how much to put down, set aside money for moving, utility setup, necessary furnishings, and initial repairs. Leave room for the savings goals and emergency cushion that fit your household. [3]

Use the inspection and your own plans to distinguish work you need to address soon from improvements that can wait. Get estimates for significant work before treating the remaining savings as available for the purchase. You want to know what your cash position will look like after the move, not just on closing day.

Make a simple homebuying budget worksheet

Use these fields for each property you seriously consider. Add the source and date beside every estimate, then update the worksheet as written information comes in.

  • Preferred monthly housing budget: $_____
  • Estimated monthly mortgage payment, including any escrow and mortgage insurance: $_____
  • Taxes, insurance, HOA dues, and other housing charges not already included above, converted to monthly amounts: $_____
  • Utilities and monthly maintenance or repair allowance: $_____
  • Total estimated monthly ownership cost: $_____
  • Purchase costs already paid: $_____
  • Cash available today: $_____
  • Additional purchase costs due before closing and not already paid: $_____
  • Estimated cash still due at closing: $_____
  • Moving and initial home expenses: $_____
  • Estimated savings after the move: $_____

Start with your current available balance. Subtract only the purchase expenses still due before closing, the estimated cash due at closing, and moving and initial home expenses. Track deposits and other expenses already paid separately for reference. Check that each remaining expense appears only once. Compare your total estimated monthly ownership cost with your preferred budget, and check that the purchase leaves the savings cushion you intended.

Compare written loan offers and update the numbers

When you receive Loan Estimates, compare the same loan amount and similar loan terms. Review the interest rate, monthly payment, loan costs, lender credits, and cash to close together. Ask about any differences in assumptions, including taxes and insurance, before deciding which offer fits. [4]

As closing approaches, compare your most recent Loan Estimate with the Closing Disclosure. Ask your lender or closing agent to explain changes and confirm the cash needed before you finalize your plans. [8]

Bring your worksheet and your unanswered questions to your next lender and real estate conversation. A useful homebuying budget should help you decide what fits your life and what needs a closer look before you make an offer.
